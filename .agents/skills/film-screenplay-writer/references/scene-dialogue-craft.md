# Scene and Dialogue Craft

Use this reference while drafting or revising screenplay pages. It does not override an approved style choice.

## Filmability

Write what performers, cameras, microphones, production design, editing, or visual effects can realize. Convert abstract explanation into observable choice, pattern, interruption, spatial behavior, object use, sound, or consequence. Retain brief internal guidance only when it gives performers playable intention rather than prescribing a fixed facial result.

Do not turn the screenplay into a shot list. Camera and coverage decisions remain downstream unless a particular image, duration, or viewpoint is essential to the story.

## Dramatic action

For each scene, know:

- what each active character is trying to make happen;
- what tactic they use and when it changes;
- what blocks, redirects, or exposes them;
- what information is withheld and who holds the advantage;
- what is different when the scene ends.

Enter after routine setup when possible and leave once the consequence is legible. A slow entrance or lingering exit is valid when duration itself carries tension, atmosphere, or emotional refusal.

## Dialogue

Treat dialogue as behavior. Distinguish:

- what the character says;
- what they want the line to do to the listener;
- what they avoid saying;
- what they are unable to say even to themselves.

Test voice through syntax, vocabulary, rhythm, social strategy, precision, evasion, humor, silence, and interruption—not accent gimmicks alone. Exposition is acceptable when a character weaponizes, bargains with, distorts, conceals, resists, or pays a cost for it.

Avoid dialogue that only explains the plot, announces the theme, names an already visible emotion, or gives every character the same polished intelligence. Preserve productive incompleteness: people may answer the threat rather than the question, seize one word and ignore the meaning, or use mundane speech around unbearable material.

## Objects, space, and sound

Use a recurring object, spatial rule, routine, sound, or verbal pattern only when its meaning can change. A payoff should transform the audience's understanding, power balance, or emotional charge rather than merely prove that the setup was remembered.

## Duration check

Compare the scene's estimated screen time with its dramatic work. Dialogue density, pauses, physical business, reaction time, and generated-shot constraints all affect duration; word count is only a rough signal. If the scene cannot fit its allotted time, simplify the action or revise the production plan instead of compressing every line into unnatural speech.

## Page review

Before delivery, check:

- the scene purpose and value movement are visible without explanatory notes;
- every important action has a clear performer and object;
- character knowledge matches canon;
- emotional change is prepared rather than switched on;
- dialogue can plausibly be spoken in the available time;
- the last beat creates consequence, not a summary;
- no new major creative decision has slipped into the draft unlabeled.
